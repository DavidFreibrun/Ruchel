[PART ONE — WHY STOCKTON]

Start with the dirt.

Stockton sits at the edge of the Sacramento–San Joaquin Delta. Rich peat soil, rivers everywhere, and in the early 1900s, some of the best asparagus land in the world.

Asparagus is brutal work. You bend to the ground, cut each spear by hand, and move to the next one. All day. Stoop labor. Growers wanted young men who could do it for weeks without stopping, and they found them.

Remember the manongs from last episode? The young men who followed the crops up the West Coast? Lettuce in Salinas, grapes in Delano, salmon canneries in Alaska every summer. Stockton was where they came back to.

Filipinos were already in Stockton by the early 1900s. By the 1920s, it was the center of Filipino life in America. From about February to May, asparagus season, as many as 15,000 Filipinos could be living in Stockton at once, packed into cheap hotels downtown.

Historians say that for much of the 20th century, Stockton had the largest community of Filipinos anywhere outside the Philippines.

And they had a name for their few blocks. About four blocks, centered on one corner, Lafayette and El Dorado.

Little Manila.

That's one.

[PART TWO — WHAT THEY BUILT]

Now picture a Saturday night there in the 1930s.

The fields are done for the week. Men who spent six days bent over asparagus rows are standing up straight, in suits. Real suits. Tailored, three pieces, the best they could afford. A lot of them saved for months to buy one. They wore them to Sunday Mass, to dinners, and to the dance halls.

Downtown Stockton had everything a lonely young man a long way from home could want. Hotels with rooms by the week. Barbershops. Pool halls. Restaurants that cooked adobo and pancit. Grocery stores. Churches. Union halls. And taxi dance halls, where you bought a ticket for a dance, usually ten cents, and danced with a woman for about a minute.

Remember what I told you last episode? Men outnumbered women by more than ten to one. Those dance floors were one of the only places a Filipino man could hold a woman's hand in public without a mob coming after him.

And they built something else. Lodges, clubs, and brotherhoods. Places with names from home, like the Rizal Social Club and the Daguhoy Lodge. When a man got sick, they took care of him. When he died with no family here, they buried him.

Why build all of that in four blocks? Because outside those four blocks, they weren't welcome.

There's a famous old photograph. A hotel sign in capital letters:

POSITIVELY NO FILIPINOS ALLOWED.

It's usually captioned "Stockton, 1930s." I have to be honest with you. Nobody's sure who took it or which hotel it was. But people who lived there said signs like that were all over town. One on the north side reportedly said "No Dogs and Filipinos Allowed."

So Little Manila wasn't just where the manongs wanted to live. For a lot of them, it was the only place they were allowed to.

And there's one more thing they built there. Power.

In 1938, asparagus workers in Stockton formed a union, the Filipino Agricultural Laborers Association. In April 1939, at the peak of the season, when the crop couldn't wait, thousands of Filipino asparagus workers walked out. Around six thousand of them.

It lasted about one day. That's all it took. The growers signed. Better pay, better terms. People still call it the Good Friday Asparagus Strike.

Think about that. Twenty-six years before Delano, the manongs of Little Manila had already shown that if Filipino workers stopped all at once, the whole valley would have to listen.

That's two.

[PART THREE — HOW IT DISAPPEARED]

Here's what I found. There's a pattern in this one too. Last episode it was recruit them, use them, shut them out. This time it's three different steps.

Call it blight. Clear it. Pave it.

Step one, call it blight.

After the war, cities all over America wanted to clean up their downtowns. Federal money was available for what they called urban renewal. But to get it, you had to show the neighborhood was a problem. Old. Crowded. Poor. A slum.

Stockton's old downtown was called the West End. And inside the West End were Stockton's Chinatown, one of the biggest in California, its Japantown, and Little Manila.

Were the buildings old? Yes. Were the people poor? A lot of them were. They were farm workers who'd been paid as little as growers could get away with for forty years. And those hotels were crowded because nobody else in town would rent to them.

But on paper, all of that became one word. Blight.

Step two, clear it.

Starting in the 1960s, the city began tearing down the West End under the West End Redevelopment Project. It didn't happen in one day. It happened block by block, year after year. Hotels, restaurants, barbershops, gone.

Researchers at UCLA went through the records. In the Asian American neighborhoods of downtown Stockton, the number of homes fell by about three-quarters between 1960 and 1970.

Three out of every four homes, gone in ten years.

And then step three. Pave it.

The state wanted a freeway across Stockton. The Crosstown Freeway. And there was more than one possible route.

Here's what the UCLA researchers found. In 1958, the state's highway planners put the route through the ethnic neighborhoods. They said the freeway would help the redevelopment project clear the so-called slums. The other route they looked at went through a mostly white neighborhood. They didn't pick that one.

The researchers also wrote that the planners knew the people in these neighborhoods didn't have the political power to fight city hall, the state, and Washington all at once.

The community fought anyway. They lost. Demolition for the freeway started in the late 1960s. By the early 1970s, the Crosstown Freeway ran right through the heart of what had been Little Manila.

So that first guess, that Little Manila just faded away? It didn't fade. It was put on a map, labeled, and knocked down. Call it blight. Clear it. Pave it.

[AFTERMATH]

And here's the part that gets me.

The families didn't just leave. In 1972, the Filipino community of Stockton pooled its money and built something new: the Filipino Plaza. Homes for families who'd been pushed out, and space for businesses. It's still there. Every August, it hosts the Barrio Fiesta.

For almost thirty years, one small piece of the old neighborhood was still standing near that corner. Then in 1999, the city came for that too, for a project called the Gateway. It tore down most of the last block of Little Manila.

That's the building from the beginning of this video. The diner belonged to Pablo Mabalon. The young woman watching was his granddaughter, Dawn Bohulano Mabalon.

She didn't just watch. That same year, she and another Stockton kid named Dillon Delvo started the Little Manila Foundation. In 2000, they got the city to name the area around Lafayette and El Dorado the Little Manila Historic Site. In 2003, the National Trust for Historic Preservation put Little Manila on its list of the eleven most endangered historic places in America.

Dawn became a historian. In 2013, she published the book on this neighborhood. It's called "Little Manila Is in the Heart." Yes, that's a nod to Carlos Bulosan, the book I told you to read last episode.

And there's one more thing about that freeway I have to tell you.

When you put a freeway through a neighborhood, the people who stay breathe it. Today, neighborhoods near Stockton's freeways have some of the highest rates of asthma emergency room visits in California.

Dawn Mabalon grew up with asthma in Stockton. In 2018, she died of an asthma attack while she was on vacation in Hawaii. She was 45.

The organization she started is called Little Manila Rising now. It still fights for the buildings. It also puts air monitors in the neighborhood, because the freeway that erased Little Manila is still there, and so are the people living next to it.

In October 2020, the city tore down the Rizal Social Club building. Today, only two of Little Manila's original buildings are still standing.

That's three.
